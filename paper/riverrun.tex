\title{\textbf{riverrun}\\[0.3em]
\large Post-Quantum Behavioral Privacy on Solana,\\ and a Ruler for What It Is Actually Worth}
\author{Manuel Guilherme Galmanus\\ \small Independent Researcher\\ \small \texttt{m.galmanus@gmail.com}}
\date{July 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
On a public ledger, hiding \emph{who} performed an action is a different problem
from hiding \emph{how much} money moved, and a harder one to reason about honestly.
This paper describes \textbf{riverrun}, a system for behavioral privacy on Solana:
a member commits an intent, and later executes exactly that intent without anyone
being able to say which member did it. The proof is a transparent, post-quantum
STARK that binds membership, a per-round nullifier, and the public action into one
object, with no trusted setup. A synchronized round of $k$ members collapses into
a single proof and a single verification.

The paper's second half is a warning aimed at riverrun and at every system like
it. Every anonymity pool advertises a number, $1/k$, that counts members. That
number is a fiction, because it ignores where the members' money came from, and
the funding graph is public. We give a ruler---the effective anonymity-set size of
Serjantov and Danezis, conditioned on funding provenance---and run it against a
live pool on Solana mainnet. A set advertising $k=30$ delivers an effective $6.5$,
and one member has an anonymity of exactly $1$. The same ruler, turned into a
pre-flight check, tells a user the anonymity \emph{they} would get in any pool
before they act. Every claim of the form ``$X$ is bound'' in the implementation
has a test that was verified to fail without the constraint it tests; three such
tests turned out to be decorative, and we say which. Where the system does not yet
hold---an unaudited hand-rolled circuit, off-chain verification, example-grade
security parameters---we say so plainly.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem, in one picture}
%==============================================================================

Imagine a town where every letter anyone ever mailed is pinned to a public board,
forever. Not the contents---those are sealed---but the envelopes: who sent what to
whom, when, and how heavy the package was. You would learn a great deal about that
town without opening a single letter. You would know who pays whom, who is
suddenly buying, who moves money at 3\,a.m., who follows whose lead an hour later.

A public blockchain is that board. People imagine that a wallet address, being a
random string rather than a name, keeps them private. The opposite is closer to
the truth. The address is a stable handle, and everything done under it accumulates
into a pattern---a \emph{behavioral fingerprint}. When you act, how much, which
protocols you touch, in what order: this is enough for modern chain-analysis to
cluster your wallets, attach a real identity, and, increasingly, to predict and
front-run what you are about to do. The tools that do this get better every month,
because the data is permanent and public and the models feeding on it are hungry.

There are two ways to want privacy here, and they are not the same. One is to hide
\emph{the money}: the amounts, the balances. The other is to hide \emph{the
behavior}: which person, out of a crowd, performed a public action. Most privacy
work on blockchains has been about the money---confidential transfers, shielded
balances, mixers that break the link between a deposit and a withdrawal of value.
This paper is about the second kind. Amounts are not hidden here and are not the
point. What we sever is the link between an \emph{actor} and an \emph{action}: the
observer sees that a swap happened, that a vote was cast, that a protocol was used,
but cannot say who did it.

Richard Feynman liked to say that if you cannot explain something simply, you do
not understand it. So here is the whole idea in one sentence, and the rest of the
paper is just this sentence made precise and made real: \emph{you disappear into a
crowd of people who all look like they could have done the thing you did}.

The name is borrowed. \emph{riverrun} is the first word of James Joyce's
\emph{Finnegans Wake}, a book with no beginning---its last sentence flows into its
first, a river returning to its own source. That image turns out to be the exact
shape of the strongest attack on systems like this, and of the defense against it,
so we kept the name and, in Section~\ref{sec:ruler}, made the circle do real work.

\section{The idea: commit an intent, execute it unlinkably}
%==============================================================================

Tornado Cash, the well-known Ethereum mixer, breaks the link between putting money
\emph{in} and taking money \emph{out}. You deposit one coin into a pool; later,
from a fresh address, you withdraw one coin, proving in zero knowledge that you are
entitled to a withdrawal without revealing which deposit was yours. The pool is a
crowd, and you are hidden in it.

riverrun keeps the shape of that trick but changes the payload. Instead of a coin,
the thing hidden is a \emph{behavior}. The flow has three steps.

\paragraph{Commit.} A member publishes a commitment
$\ell = \rescue(s, a)$ into a public set, where $s$ is a secret only they know and
$a$ is the action they intend to perform---``swap $1$ SOL for USDC on venue V'',
say, encoded as a hash. Publishing $\ell$ announces, to everyone, that
\emph{somebody in this set intends action $a$}, while revealing nothing about who.
The set is a Merkle tree; its root is the public fingerprint of the whole crowd.

\paragraph{Execute.} Later---ideally in a synchronized round where many members
perform the same action at the same moment---the member performs $a$ from a fresh
identity, accompanied by a zero-knowledge proof of a single sentence:
\begin{quote}
\emph{``I know a secret $s$ whose commitment $\rescue(s,a)$ is a leaf under the
public root $R$, and my nullifier for this round is $n = \rescue(s, r)$.''}
\end{quote}
The action $a$ is public---the observer sees it happen. The author is not.

\paragraph{Settle.} The pool verifies the proof, checks that the nullifier $n$ has
not been seen this round, records it as spent, and releases the action. The public
transcript is just $\{R, a, n\}$, with no link back to any commitment.

Two questions decide whether this is privacy or theater, and both are about the
nullifier. First, why have one at all? Because without it, a member could execute
their action a thousand times from a thousand fresh identities, and the ``crowd''
would be one person wearing a thousand masks. The nullifier is a token, derived
from the secret and the round, that can be spent exactly once per round. It is
\emph{unlinkable}: $\rescue(s, r)$ reveals nothing about $s$, and the nullifiers a
single member produces in different rounds, $\rescue(s, r_1)$ and $\rescue(s,
r_2)$, cannot be tied to each other. So it enforces ``one action per member per
round'' without ever identifying the member. This is a known and well-studied
primitive; PLUME~\cite{plume} formalizes exactly this kind of deterministic
nullifier and proves its uniqueness and secrecy.

Second, and this is the subtle one: how do we know the nullifier $n$ that a member
reveals really came from the \emph{same} secret that proved membership? If those
two facts---``I am a member'' and ``my nullifier is $n$''---are proven separately,
a member could prove membership honestly and then present a nullifier belonging to
somebody else, spending a stranger's turn or hiding that they have already spent
their own. The two halves must be welded together inside one proof. Making that
weld correct, and knowing it is correct, is the technical heart of the next
section.

\section{The proof: one object, three bindings}
\label{sec:proof}
%==============================================================================

A zero-knowledge proof lets you convince someone that a statement is true while
revealing nothing beyond its truth. There are two broad families in use on
blockchains today, and the choice between them is a genuine fork with real costs
on each side.

The first, pairing-based SNARKs such as Groth16, produce tiny proofs---a couple of
hundred bytes---that verify cheaply. Their price is a \emph{trusted setup}: before
anyone can prove anything, a one-time ceremony must generate a structured
``proving key'', and if the secret randomness of that ceremony is ever recovered,
the whole system can be forged. For a circuit as large as a Merkle-path check, that
key is tens of megabytes that must be produced, trusted, and shipped to every
prover. And the security rests on the hardness of discrete logarithms over
elliptic curves---which a large enough quantum computer breaks.

The second family, STARKs, are built from nothing but a hash function. No pairings,
no elliptic curves, no ceremony, no per-circuit setup. Their security rests on the
collision-resistance of a hash, which is believed to survive a quantum adversary.
The price is proof size: a STARK is kilobytes, not bytes. riverrun takes this side
of the trade, deliberately, and Section~\ref{sec:cost} measures exactly what it
costs and what it buys.

\subsection{What the circuit proves}

Concretely, riverrun uses a STARK over a Rescue-Prime~\cite{rescueprime} Merkle
tree---an ``arithmetization-oriented'' hash chosen because its structure is cheap
to express as the polynomial constraints a STARK understands. The proof's public
inputs are the four-tuple
\[
\{\,R,\; n,\; r,\; a\,\},
\]
the root, the nullifier, the round, and the action. Its private inputs---the
witness, which the proof hides---are the secret $s$, the leaf's position in the
tree, and the Merkle path. A single secret must satisfy all of the following at
once:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.15em]
\item \textbf{Membership.} $\rescue(s, a)$ is a leaf under the public root $R$,
      via the private path. (The member is genuinely in the set.)
\item \textbf{Nullifier binding.} $n = \rescue(s, r)$: the revealed nullifier is
      this member's, for this round.
\item \textbf{Action binding.} The very leaf that resolves to $R$ commits to the
      \emph{public} action $a$. (The member executes the intent they registered,
      not a different one.)
\end{enumerate}
Because all three read from the same private $s$, no member can mix and match:
prove membership as Alice while spending Bob's nullifier, or prove they committed
action $a$ while executing action $a'$. That is the weld.

\begin{intuition}
\textbf{What a STARK actually is, in one paragraph.} Write your computation as a
big table---rows are time-steps, columns are registers---so that ``the computation
was done correctly'' becomes ``every adjacent pair of rows obeys a fixed rule''.
Turn each column into a polynomial that passes through its values. The rules become
equations these polynomials must satisfy. Now the prover commits to the
polynomials (with a Merkle tree of their values on a larger domain) and the
verifier challenges them at random points; a cheat would have to make a
\emph{wrong} low-degree polynomial agree with a right one almost everywhere, which
is impossible unless they are equal. FRI is the machinery that lets the verifier
check ``this really is a low-degree polynomial'' by repeatedly folding it in half
and spot-checking. The disanalogy with a normal exam: the verifier never sees the
answers, only a few random cells and a proof that all the cells are consistent---
which is why the witness stays hidden.
\end{intuition}

In riverrun's case the table has width nine
(Figure~\ref{fig:trace}): six columns hold the Rescue hash state, one holds a
Merkle direction bit, and two---the \emph{carry}---hold the secret across the one
hash cycle that computes the nullifier before the Merkle path begins. A single
constraint at the first row, the ``start-tie'', forces the nullifier's input to
equal the value the path will hash. Delete that one constraint and the weld comes
apart.

\subsection{Knowing the weld holds}

A privacy proof that is subtly wrong is worse than no proof, because it advertises
a guarantee it does not deliver. So the interesting question is not ``did we write
the constraints'' but ``how do we know they bite''. The answer used throughout
riverrun is \emph{mutation testing}: for every claim of the form ``$X$ is bound'',
there is a test, and we delete the constraint that is supposed to enforce $X$ and
confirm the test then fails. If deleting the constraint changes nothing, the test
was not testing the constraint---it was passing for some other reason---and it is
worthless.

This is not a formality. Three tests written during development turned out to be
exactly that kind of worthless, and mutation testing is how they were caught.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.2em]
\item The first tests for the \emph{action} binding checked that announcing a
      different public action was rejected. They passed even with the binding
      constraint deleted---because the action feeds the proof's Fiat--Shamir
      transcript, so a mismatched public input is rejected anyway, for a reason
      having nothing to do with the constraint under test. The real attack, which
      the useful test performs, is to hash the committed action into the leaf (so
      the Merkle path still resolves) while \emph{announcing} a different one. With
      the constraint deleted, that forged proof verifies. With it, nothing usable
      comes out.
\item The nullifier-binding negative tests that only varied public inputs passed
      on the boundary assertions alone, with no start-tie at all. The test that
      actually guards the weld builds the trace an attacker would want---hash
      secret $A$, carry member $B$ into the path---and only the start-tie stops it.
\item In the ricorso extension (Section~\ref{sec:ricorso}) a whole check---that the
      revealed migration nullifier comes from the migrating secret---had \emph{no}
      test at all until a mutation left every test green and exposed the gap.
\end{itemize}

None of this makes the circuit audited. It is hand-rolled, and a passing negative
test is necessary but not sufficient for soundness; an independent review is on
the roadmap and is named as an open item, not glossed. What mutation testing buys
is the removal of a specific, common, and embarrassing failure: tests that look
like they prove something and prove nothing.

\subsection{A leak we found by measuring}
\label{sub:leak}

One more story, because it is the kind of thing that separates a system that was
measured from one that was merely asserted. The first version of the circuit
carried the secret in two ``carry'' columns held \emph{constant} across the whole
execution trace. This looked harmless. It was not. A STARK proof works by opening
the prover's committed data at random points, and a column that is constant has a
constant low-degree extension---so the secret appeared, verbatim, in essentially
every opening. We only knew because we wrote a test that checked, over many proofs,
whether the raw bytes of the secret appeared in the proof. They did: twenty times
out of twenty. Confining the carry to the eight rows where it is actually needed
fixed it: zero out of twenty. A single-sample test would have missed it. This is
also why we are careful to call the result ``the witness is not on the wire'' and
not ``zero-knowledge''---the underlying prover does not randomize the witness, so
formal zero-knowledge is a claim we decline to make.

\section{The synchronized round: one crowd, one proof}
\label{sec:round}
%==============================================================================

The privacy argument rests on a crowd. If $k$ members perform the same action in
the same round, an observer's chance of attributing any one execution to its author
is at best $1/k$. The natural way to run this is a \emph{synchronized round}: many
members acting identically at one moment, so that there is no timing, no ordering,
nothing to separate them.

Here is a small observation with a large payoff. That synchronized round is exactly
the structure that lets many proofs collapse into one. A STARK proof is a statement
about an execution trace---a long table of numbers. Proving $k$ members separately
means $k$ traces, $k$ proofs, and, on-chain, $k$ verifications. But the $k$
sub-traces are independent, so they can be laid end to end into one longer trace,
with the periodic constraints that fire ``once per member'' repeating along it, and
a \emph{seam} constraint that switches off the linking logic at each boundary so
one member's last row does not bleed into the next member's first. The result is
one proof for the whole round, and one verification.

The savings are not marginal. Table~\ref{tab:batch} gives the measured numbers.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{rrrr}
\toprule
members $k$ & one batched proof & $k$ separate proofs & saving \\
\midrule
$4$  & $19{,}772$ B & $45{,}300$ B    & $2.3\times$ \\
$64$ & $43{,}684$ B & $1{,}074{,}169$ B & $24.6\times$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Batching a synchronized round. At $k=64$ a per-member scheme ships over a
megabyte of proof and needs $64$ verifications; the batched round is one proof and
one verification.}
\label{tab:batch}
\end{table}

Honesty requires two caveats, both structural. First, the prover of a batched round
must know every member's secret. For a crowd of mutually distrustful strangers that
is not privacy---it is a custodian, and batching there would need distributed
proving. But for a single operator running $k$ identities---an agent fleet, a
market maker with many wallets, precisely an audience this problem serves---one
prover holds all $k$ secrets already, and the $24.6\times$ is real. Second, batching
trades prover time for proof size: the batched trace is $k$ times longer, and
proving is super-linear, so the round costs more wall-clock to prove than the sum
of the individual proofs. It is the right trade when what you are paying for is
on-chain verifications, which is the scarce resource.

\section{The ricorso: the crowd is reborn}
\label{sec:ricorso}
%==============================================================================

Now the name earns its keep. \emph{Finnegans Wake} is built on Giambattista Vico's
cycle of history: ages that repeat, and a fourth age, the \emph{ricorso}, the
return that begins the whole thing again. riverrun already borrows the book's
circularity once, for the funding graph in Section~\ref{sec:ruler}. The ricorso is
the other borrowing, and it closes a leak that the basic construction has and that
is easy to miss.

Here is the leak. A member's per-round nullifier changes every round, which unlinks
one execution from another. But their \emph{leaf}---$\rescue(s,a)$---never changes.
It is published once at commit and stays in the tree forever. Two consequences
follow, neither of them stated in the system as described so far. Anyone who ever
learns your leaf, through a bug or a subpoena or a careless client, can link you
across every round you have ever acted in, past and future---the per-round
nullifiers protect executions from each other, but nothing protects a member from
their own history. And a set that only ever grows is itself a record: join-order is
public, so the set is a timeline of who arrived when.

The ricorso fixes both by letting the set be \emph{reborn} each cycle. A member
holds a fresh secret per cycle, derived from one master secret:
\[
s_c = \rescue(s, c, \textsf{dom}_{\text{cycle}}),
\qquad
\ell_c = \rescue(s_c, a),
\]
and at each rebirth publishes the new leaf $\ell_c$ while proving, in zero
knowledge, that it descends from \emph{some} leaf under the previous cycle's root---
without revealing which. A migration nullifier
$\rescue(s, c, \textsf{dom}_{\text{migrate}})$, spent once per cycle, stops one
seat from becoming several at the rebirth. The two domain tags must differ; if they
did not, publishing a migration nullifier would leak the next cycle's secret, and
with it the member's next leaf, making the rebirth public and pointless---a
requirement the implementation pins with a test. History stops accumulating,
because the leaves an observer can see this cycle are unlinkable to the leaves they
saw last cycle.

In the current implementation this relation is specified and checked ``in the
clear''---a function that takes the witness and returns whether the migration is
valid, with mutation-tested coverage of each of its four checks---but the STARK
that proves it \emph{without} the witness is designed and not yet built. The reason
is a concrete and slightly annoying interaction: the migration circuit needs five
hash cycles before the Merkle path, which shifts the valid anonymity-set sizes to
$\{8, 2048\}$, while the execution circuit needs sizes $\{4, 64, 16384\}$, and the
two do not intersect, so shipping the migration proof also means padding the
execution circuit to realign the two. That is a day of careful work on the most
delicate code in the system, and the honest place to stop, with the execution path
green and tested, is at the specification---exactly where the nullifier binding
itself stopped before it was later built and mutation-verified.

%==============================================================================
\section{The ruler: your $k$ is not your $k$}
\label{sec:ruler}
%==============================================================================

Everything so far has been about building the pool. This section is about
measuring it, and it applies not just to riverrun but to every anonymity pool of
this kind. It is, we think, the most useful part of the paper, because it names a
number that is quietly wrong across an entire category of systems.

\subsection{The advertised number counts the wrong thing}

Every pool reports its privacy as $1/k$: there are $k$ members, so an observer has
a one-in-$k$ chance of guessing the actor. That counts \emph{members}. It assumes
the members are interchangeable---that knowing anything about one tells you nothing
about which one acted.

They are not interchangeable, because of where their money came from. Every member
had to fund their wallet somehow, and on a public ledger the funding trail is
public. Trace a member's wallet backward and you often reach an attributable
origin: a major exchange's withdrawal address, a known service, a doxxed funder.
Now sort the whole set by these origins. If the acting identity's origin is
observable too---and a fresh withdrawal wallet has to be funded from
\emph{somewhere}---then learning the actor's origin does not leave $k$ suspects. It
leaves only the members who share that origin. The crowd you are actually hidden in
is your \emph{provenance class}, not the whole set.

This is not a new idea in the abstract. Serjantov and Danezis~\cite{serjantov2002}
showed in 2002 that the honest measure of anonymity is not the size of the set but
the \emph{entropy} of the distribution linking suspects to the event---the
``effective anonymity-set size''. And measuring the gap between a mixer's
advertised and true anonymity is an established line of work on
Ethereum~\cite{tutela,beres,crosschain,du}. Two things are new here: the channel
(funding provenance, which those works do not condition on---they use address
reuse, timing, wallet fingerprints) and the chain (none of it has been run on
Solana).

\subsection{The formula, and why its direction matters}

Partition the $k$ members into provenance classes of sizes $n_1, n_2, \dots$. The
adversary learns the actor's class $c$ with probability $n_c / k$, and is then left
with a uniform guess among that class's $n_c$ members. The residual uncertainty is
the average, over which class the actor came from, of the log of the class size:
\begin{equation}
H \;=\; \sum_c \frac{n_c}{k}\,\log_2 n_c,
\qquad
\keff \;=\; 2^{H}.
\label{eq:effk}
\end{equation}
$\keff$ is the size of the crowd the member is genuinely hidden in. One class
holding everyone gives back $\log_2 k$---nothing was partitioned, the set is worth
its advertised size. All-singleton classes give $H=0$, an effective $k$ of one:
every member individually identified.

\begin{intuition}
\textbf{The formula, worked by hand.} Take a pool of ten members. Suppose the
funding trace sorts them into three provenance classes: six came from exchange A,
three from exchange B, and one from a wallet all its own. The advertised anonymity
is $1/10$. But condition on the actor's origin. With probability $6/10$ the actor
is an ``A'' member, and then the adversary cannot tell which of the six---that is
$\log_2 6 \approx 2.58$ bits. With probability $3/10$ it is a ``B'' member,
$\log_2 3 \approx 1.58$ bits. With probability $1/10$ it is the lone member, and
the adversary knows exactly who: $\log_2 1 = 0$ bits. Average them:
\[
H = \tfrac{6}{10}(2.58) + \tfrac{3}{10}(1.58) + \tfrac{1}{10}(0) = 2.02\ \text{bits},
\qquad \keff = 2^{2.02} \approx 4.1.
\]
The pool sold ten and delivered four. And the lone member has an anonymity of
exactly one: for them the pool is a mirror with their name on it, no matter how big
the crowd behind them.
\end{intuition}

The direction of this formula is the whole thing, and the first implementation had
it backwards. It is tempting to measure how \emph{concentrated} the classes are---
the min-entropy of the class distribution---but that reports catastrophe precisely
when the measurement found \emph{nothing} to partition on. A single class of seven,
which is the happy case (no origins found, everyone alike), came out as ``effective
$k = 1$'' under the wrong formula, when the honest reading is ``effective $k = 7$''.
Following Serjantov and Danezis, and against the normalized variant of D\'iaz et
al.\ (which scores a set of one as a perfect $1.0$), we use the residual entropy of
Equation~\ref{eq:effk} directly. Four unit tests pin the direction, and one of them
caught the arithmetic a second time.

\subsection{The measurement}

We ran this against a live Tornado-style SOL privacy pool on Solana mainnet,
sampling $30$ real depositors and tracing each one's funding graph backward with a
bounded, public-RPC walk. Table~\ref{tab:effk} is the result.

\centering

\textbf{worst case} & $\mathbf{1}$ \\

\end{table}

Three honesties about this number. It is not a flaw in that particular pool: no
deposit-pool design can control where its users' money came from, which is exactly
why the channel goes unmeasured and keeps working as an attack. It is a
\emph{floor}, not a verdict: a public RPC following only SOL transfers to bounded
depth sees less than a funded analyst with a maintained tag database and token-flow
tracing, so the true leak is at least this large. And $37\%$ is reassuringly in the
same range---$20$ to $35\%$---that the Ethereum literature~\cite{crosschain} finds
with behavioral heuristics on Tornado Cash, which is a sanity check on the tool: a
result of $95\%$ would have been a reason to distrust the instrument, not the pool.

\subsection{The same ruler, turned on riverrun---and on the user}

Measuring other people's pools and not your own is marketing. The same function
runs over riverrun's own constructions. Against a synthetic population of $2000$
members, the ``rooted decoy'' construction that the field ships is worth an
effective $500$; the circular, origin-free construction---the funding cycle with no
beginning, the \emph{Finnegans Wake} image made numeric
(Figure~\ref{fig:cycle})---is worth the full $2000$.

That is the circularity defense stated as a number instead of a metaphor, with the
honest caveat the tool prints: a cycle with no attributable origin holds only while
its funding sources are themselves unattributable; one known exchange entry
degrades it from ``no origin'' to ``ambiguous origin'', which is still a large win
and a smaller, measured one.

The most useful turn of the ruler, though, is toward the user. The measurement
above audits a pool after the fact, which helps auditors. Flip it: before a user
deposits into \emph{any} pool, trace \emph{their} wallet's funding graph, sample
the pool's current depositors, and tell them the anonymity \emph{they personally}
would get there. The tool is protocol-agnostic---it reads public chain data, not
any pool's internals---so it works against a program it has never seen. It returns
one of three verdicts, each with an action:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.15em]
\item \textbf{Exposed}: you would be alone in your provenance class. The pool's size
      is irrelevant to you; fund a fresh wallet from a source other depositors also
      use, or wait for a same-origin crowd.
\item \textbf{Weak}: only a small minority shares your class; your real anonymity is
      far below the headline.
\item \textbf{OK}: a healthy fraction shares your class, so the crowd is genuinely
      yours---within the trace bound, meaning ``no cheap attribution found'', never
      ``anonymous''.
\end{itemize}
This is where the leverage is. Auditing pools reaches the handful of people who
audit pools. Protecting a user at the moment they act reaches every user of every
privacy protocol on the chain.

\section{Cost, and the on-chain reality}
\label{sec:cost}
%==============================================================================

A privacy tool nobody can afford to run is a paper, not a tool. So we measured.

\subsection{What one execution costs}

Table~\ref{tab:cost} gives the prover and verifier cost of a single execution,
across anonymity-set sizes.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{rrrrr}
\toprule
set size & proof & prove & verify & setup artifacts \\
\midrule
$4$      & $11{,}929$ B & $2.0$ ms & $0.20$ ms & $0$ bytes \\
$64$     & $17{,}045$ B & $3.7$ ms & $0.35$ ms & $0$ bytes \\
$16{,}384$ & $19{,}064$ B & $8.1$ ms & $0.43$ ms & $0$ bytes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{One execution, measured. An anonymity set of $16{,}384$ members proves in
$8$ milliseconds. Proof size grows logarithmically: $4096\times$ the members costs
$1.6\times$ the proof.}
\label{tab:cost}
\end{table}

The column that decides whether any of this is deployable is the last one. A
pairing-based prover needs a proving key produced by a ceremony and shipped to
every client---for a Merkle circuit of this depth, tens of megabytes that must
exist, be trusted, and be distributed before anyone proves anything. riverrun's
prover is the code; there is nothing to distribute and nothing to trust. For the
audience this serves---agents, market makers, ordinary users proving before each
action---that is the difference between a tool that ships and one that needs an
install.

\subsection{Verifying on-chain: measured, and honest about the wall}

The hard part is on-chain verification. If the chain itself checks the proof, no
one has to be trusted; if it does not, someone does. So we built a Solana program
that runs the real Winterfell verifier---FRI folding, Merkle openings, Fiat--Shamir,
constraint evaluation---inside the runtime, and measured it.

It runs. It costs $3.77$ million compute units at $k=4$ and $7.13$ million at
$k=64$. Both exceed Solana's $1.4$-million-CU limit for a single transaction, so
this needs execution chunked across transactions---which existing
work~\cite{mosaic} implements with a resumable verifier. The number is consistent
with the published Solana STARK-verification study~\cite{onchainstark}, which
verifies a Winterfell proof at $1.1$M CU for a $4.4$\,KB proof: we spend $3.4\times$
the compute for a $2.7\times$ larger proof.

Then we asked the sharp question: can tuning the parameters bring even the smallest
set under the $1.4$M limit? We swept the number of FRI queries down to the floor:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rr}
\toprule
queries & CU ($k=4$) \\
\midrule
$28$ & $3{,}768{,}496$ \\
$16$ & $2{,}769{,}318$ \\
$8$  & $1{,}951{,}812$ \\
$4$  & $1{,}567{,}787$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Even at four queries---a security level no one would ship---it is $1.57$M CU, still
over the cap. The base cost of setup, out-of-domain sampling, and constraint
evaluation over a $128$-bit field dominates, and the blow-up factor cannot go below
eight because the Rescue constraint degree is five. So single-transaction
verification is not a tuning problem. It is a \emph{field} problem, and that is now
a measurement rather than an argument.

This points cleanly at the fix. Our proof is large, and our verification is
expensive, for the same reason: the $128$-bit field, where every element is sixteen
bytes and every multiplication is several $64$-bit operations. A Circle STARK over
the $31$-bit Mersenne field M31---the modern choice, used in production
provers~\cite{stwo} and already demonstrated as an on-chain Solana verifier at
$\sim 31$k CU~\cite{murkl}, post-quantum, no ceremony---would shrink both. Porting
riverrun's circuit to a small field is the concrete roadmap, and it is a field
change, not a possibility proof.

\subsection{What the chain enforces today}

Until that port, the deployed on-chain program does not verify the proof itself. It
would be dishonest to leave that implicit, so the program makes the trust explicit
and bounded rather than hidden. The pool names a \emph{verifier} key; an execution
must carry that key's signature over exactly the tuple being settled, checked
through the runtime's native signature precompile and instruction introspection.
This removes the two cheap attacks---an arbitrary signer settling an action, and a
watcher front-running a nullifier out of the mempool---because neither can produce
the verifier's signature over their own tuple. It does \emph{not} remove trust: a
dishonest verifier can attest to a proof that does not exist. It is a named
assumption, not a proof, and the roadmap above is how it goes away. The program
also prices a seat in the set (an entry fee, so inflating $k$ costs money rather
than nothing) and refuses to settle below a minimum-anonymity floor---anti-Sybil
that is honestly ``priced, not prevented'', with the stronger
one-nullifier-per-verifier construction~\cite{selfcred} named as the real fix.

%==============================================================================
\section{What does not hold yet}
%==============================================================================

A paper that only lists its strengths is an advertisement. Here, in one place, is
what riverrun does not yet do, so that no reader has to discover it by accident.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.25em]
\item \textbf{The circuit is hand-rolled and unaudited.} Mutation testing removes a
      class of worthless tests, but a passing negative test is necessary, not
      sufficient, for soundness. An independent review is required before any
      production claim.
\item \textbf{It is not formally zero-knowledge.} The underlying prover does not
      randomize the witness, so the honest claim is ``the secret does not appear in
      the transmitted proof'', checked empirically, not proven.
\item \textbf{Verification is off-chain today}, behind the named-verifier
      attestation above. The chain does not yet check the proof; the measured cost
      and the M31 path (Section~\ref{sec:cost}) are how that changes.
\item \textbf{Security parameters are example-grade}, roughly $84$ bits, not the
      $128$ a deployment needs.
\item \textbf{``Rootless'' provenance is conditional.} The circularity defense holds
      only while a construction's funding sources are themselves unattributable;
      one known exchange entry degrades it, measurably.
\item \textbf{The ricorso proof is specified, not built}, for the set-size-alignment
      reason in Section~\ref{sec:ricorso}.
\item \textbf{Anti-Sybil is priced, not prevented.} An attacker with money can still
      buy $k$; the fee raises the cost, and the funding graph makes the cheap
      version of the attack visible to the very ruler of Section~\ref{sec:ruler}.
\end{itemize}

The whole implementation is Rust, MIT-licensed, with $74$ passing tests across the
cryptographic core, the STARK, the pool, and the on-chain program, and clean
static analysis. Every measurement in this paper is reproducible from the
repository with a single command.

%==============================================================================
\section{The threat model, stated plainly}
%==============================================================================

It is easy to claim privacy against an attacker you never describe. Here is the one
riverrun is built against, and the one it is not.

\textbf{What the adversary can do.} They see the entire public ledger, forever:
every transaction, every amount, every address, and the full funding graph behind
any wallet. They run modern chain-analysis---clustering, timing, graph learning---
and they may maintain a tag database that labels exchange and service addresses.
They observe the pool's public transcript $\{R, a, n\}$ for every execution. For the
funding-graph results (Section~\ref{sec:ruler}) they can additionally observe the
provenance class of the \emph{acting} identity---which is cheap, because a fresh
withdrawal wallet must be funded from somewhere visible.

\textbf{What riverrun defends.} The link between an actor and their action. Given an
execution, the adversary should do no better than $1/\keff$ at naming the member who
authored it, where $\keff$ is the effective size of that member's provenance class,
not the advertised set size---and the tools in this paper measure $\keff$ rather
than assume it.

\textbf{What riverrun does \emph{not} defend, by design.} Amounts and balances---
they are public here, and hiding them is a different, composable problem. The value
layer of consensus: the ledger records that value moved one way, and no circularity
argument changes that; the circularity lives on the attribution layer an analyst
reads, not the settlement layer consensus enforces. And, in the deployed program
today, the correctness of the membership proof itself, which is delegated to a named
verifier (Section~\ref{sec:cost}) until on-chain verification lands.

The reason to write this down is that a privacy claim without a threat model is not
falsifiable, and an unfalsifiable claim is not an engineering claim. Every number in
this paper is stated against this adversary.

%==============================================================================
\section{Related work}
%==============================================================================

riverrun's \emph{goal}---hiding which member of a set acted---is anonymous
authentication, a mature field. PrivDID~\cite{privdid} states the same session-
unlinkability goal; anonymous-credential frameworks with predicate
proofs~\cite{predproofs,re2creds} live in the same threat model of a verifier who
wants to correlate sessions; PLUME~\cite{plume} formalizes the deterministic
nullifier riverrun uses. The \emph{metric} is Serjantov and
Danezis~\cite{serjantov2002}. The \emph{programme} of measuring advertised-versus-
true anonymity is established on Ethereum~\cite{tutela,beres,crosschain,du}. And
on-chain STARK verification on Solana has been done, including with the same
Winterfell library~\cite{onchainstark,murkl,mosaic}.

What is narrow and new here is the combination: the payload is behavior rather than
value, which is what the target problem asks for; the funding graph is
\emph{measured} rather than assumed away, on riverrun's own constructions as well
as on live third-party pools; and that measurement is turned into a user-facing,
protocol-agnostic pre-flight defense that, to our knowledge, did not previously
exist on Solana.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%==============================================================================

There are two ideas in this paper, and they are stronger together than apart.

The first is a way to hide behavior, not money, on a public chain: commit an
intent, execute it unlinkably, and prove the whole thing with a transparent,
post-quantum STARK that welds membership, nullifier, and action into one object, at
a cost we measured down to the compute unit. It has real, named limits---an
unaudited circuit, off-chain verification today, a field that is too big---and a
concrete path through each.

The second is a ruler for whether any such system actually works, applied first and
hardest to riverrun itself. Every anonymity pool on the chain advertises a number
that overstates its privacy, because it counts members and ignores where their
money came from, and the funding graph is public. We measured the gap on a live
pool---advertised thirty, effective six and a half, worst case one---and turned the
ruler into a tool that protects a user before they act, against any pool.

The honest version of a privacy system is one that ships its own strongest attack
and reports what it finds. That is what we tried to build. In the words the system
takes its name from: \emph{a way a lone last a loved a long the riverrun}---the
river returns to its source, and so should every claim, back to the measurement
that supports it.
